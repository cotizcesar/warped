Distributed systems are foundational to modern computing. At their core, they coordinate multiple independent computers that appear to users as a single coherent service. These systems must handle partial failures gracefully: any individual node can crash, messages can be delayed or lost, and network partitions can split the cluster into isolated groups that cannot communicate. The eight fallacies of distributed computing, articulated by Peter Deutsch and others at Sun Microsystems in the 1990s, enumerate assumptions developers often make that turn out to be false in production. The network is reliable, latency is zero, bandwidth is infinite, the network is secure, topology does not change, there is one administrator, transport cost is zero, and the network is homogeneous. Each of these assumptions fails in real deployments, so robust distributed software is designed defensively with retries, timeouts, idempotency, circuit breakers, and backpressure. The CAP theorem, proposed by Eric Brewer in 2000, states that during a network partition a distributed data store can provide either consistency or availability but not both. CP systems sacrifice availability to keep replicas strongly consistent; AP systems sacrifice strong consistency to remain responsive. Modern systems like Spanner and CockroachDB aim to deliver both by leveraging synchronized clocks, but they pay a latency cost. Consensus algorithms such as Paxos and Raft allow a cluster of nodes to agree on a sequence of values even in the presence of failures, but they are notoriously difficult to implement correctly. Raft was designed to be understandable; it elects a leader for a term, replicates the leader's log entries to followers, and commits entries when a majority have acknowledged them. The leader sends periodic heartbeats; if a follower stops hearing from the leader it becomes a candidate and requests votes from peers. If a candidate receives votes from a majority it becomes the new leader for the next term. Logs are matched entry by entry and any divergence is resolved by overwriting follower entries with the leader's. Snapshots compact the log so that a slow follower can recover quickly by streaming a recent snapshot rather than replaying every entry. Production Raft implementations such as etcd and Consul are battle-tested at scale, but the surface area of distributed systems engineering remains vast and filled with subtle correctness pitfalls. End-to-end testing, chaos engineering, and observability are the disciplines that turn a theoretically sound protocol into a reliable production system that operators trust and that users depend upon every day.
